# System Paper Writing

A systems paper is a research account: a problem that matters in the development of a field, the ideas that address it, the system as a concrete construction that develops those ideas, and evidence for what the construction achieves. A system's units are modules, documents, releases, and the order in which decisions were made; none of those is the paper's unit.

Underneath the account, the paper makes **claims**, and claims are the unit for *checking* it. Keep these two representations apart, because conflating them is the root of most failed drafts:

- **The reader's narrative** runs from the field to the construction: motivation → the field's progress → research problem → ideas → the system as their construction → evidence.
- **The writer's analysis** runs from the system toward claims: system → decisions → candidate claims → frame → claim/evidence map. The tests, ledgers, and simulations in this skill belong here. They change the work (the size of a claim, an experiment, a scope); they do not appear in the prose.

Write the analysis order as the narrative and the paper becomes design-led; write the checks into the prose and it becomes defensive.

This skill was distilled from two sources. The first is a study of how classic papers were actually made from their systems (MapReduce, Spark/RDD, TensorFlow whitepaper→OSDI, PyTorch, TVM, XLA/JAX, Ray, Pathways, GFS, HDFS, Bigtable, Ceph, Dynamo, Raft, Paxos Made Live, Spanner, Borg/Omega, vLLM, FlashAttention, AlphaTensor, AlphaDev, FunSearch, AlphaEvolve), comparing each paper against its code, docs, earlier versions, retrospectives, and critiques. The second is an author's reviews of successive agent-written drafts of one systems paper, which showed how agents fail at the constructive side of the work.

There are three failure poles, and the work is steering between them:

- **Catalog**: the outline mirrors the repo tree or the design documents; each component gets a section at uniform depth; defaults without reasons. A paper whose sections carry concept titles but follow the design package's own order is still a catalog: a design-led retelling. (HDFS's paper, the TensorFlow whitepaper, most design docs.)
- **Vapor**: the concept is stated but no mechanism is concrete enough for a reader to re-implement the key idea. Its manifesto form defines concepts, derives requirements from them, and shows the design satisfying them, while the problem is never grounded in the field. (Pathways' motivating workloads; AlphaEvolve's one-sentence program database.)
- **Defense**: the paper spends its attention protecting claims instead of developing them. Disclaimers, repeated statements of what the work is not, a cycle of claim → evidence-hunt → qualification → definition, signposting about how to read the paper, a related-work section that dismisses each system by one missing property. It arises when this skill's checks are written into the prose instead of being used to size the claims.

The target: a reader who has never seen the codebase comes away knowing the problem and why it matters now, the idea that addresses it, the few mechanisms that make the capabilities possible, the evidence, and the conditions under which it works.

**Where to enter.** Starting from a system with no paper: work through stages 1–9 below. Holding a draft: read it against stage 9, then return to the upstream stage the symptoms trace to, usually 2, 3, or 4. If the draft's conception is wrong (design-led, deduction-driven, defensive throughout, or addressing the wrong problem), re-conceive from stage 2 rather than revising: treat the old draft as evidence of what failed and carry over facts, not its sentences, structure, examples, or coined terms. Reviewing someone else's paper: use stage 9 and `references/diagnostics.md`, separating what the draft alone shows from what only its authors can answer. Asked only to polish prose: check first that the frame holds (a problem grounded in the field, a thesis stated without the system's name or internal terms, evidence for the headline claim); if it doesn't, say so before polishing.

## Core stance

These are the few judgments everything else follows from. Each is a claim about what made papers work or fail, not a rule to obey blindly.

1. **The contribution is chosen, not found.** The same Spark code was "a framework for working sets" (2010), "a fault-tolerant distributed memory abstraction" (2012), and "a unified execution model" (2016). Choose under three constraints that must hold together: it is *true of the system* (it actually explains the capabilities); it is *carried by evidence the authors can produce*; and it *changes what a competent reader in the field can do or believes*, measured against the field's actual state. HDFS fails the third; Ray's "stitching specialist systems is untenable" fails the second.

2. **Start from the field's problem; know what the work changes.** A strong paper takes a problem the field is actually working on, understood through what existing work achieved and what those achievements made possible, and changes something specific. It may dissolve a tradeoff (PyTorch: usability without giving up speed), recast the quantity to optimize (FlashAttention: memory traffic rather than FLOPs), meet a requirement that was unmet (Dynamo's 99.9th-percentile SLA), or revise assumptions for a new environment (GFS). Find that change in the literature as it is; do not construct a foil. If the change can only be stated as "prior systems all lack X", or nobody can be cited as holding the current view, the problem has not been found yet. If the only change you can state is "system X is slower than ours", you have a benchmark result, not a contribution.

3. **Depth follows argumentative load, not effort or code size.** vLLM's 273-line block manager carries the paper; its roughly 8.4K-line model executor gets one sentence. MapReduce spends about 1.5 pages on its model and 4.5 on the runtime and refinements because the runtime is where the claim is demonstrated. The rule is not "give the novel thing the most pages"; it is "make the other sections depend on the novel thing." Dependence is a relevance test, not a deductive structure: the paper can leave questions open.

4. **Put the thesis at the most stable layer the evidence supports.** Interfaces, formulations, metrics, invariants, and requirements outlive the implementations that first realized them (Ray's task/actor API survived its control plane being inverted; "IO-aware" outlived FlashAttention's Algorithm 1; RADOS outlived Ceph's MDS-first framing). Present the implementation as one realization of the idea, and do not omit it: every surviving concept was believed because a working mechanism demonstrated it.

5. **Every claim has evidence with a known status; every figure answers a claim.** The claims that later got attacked were exactly the ones that were asserted rather than measured (Ray's untested unification, Dynamo's undefined "consistency window", Spark's 20×, which the paper's own breakdown attributes to avoided HDFS reads, parsing, and deserialization rather than to lineage). Results not yet measured are not written as results.

6. **Bounded generality is credible.** State the conditions under which the idea works as part of the result (RDD's "Applications not suitable for RDDs", vLLM's "does not help training", FunSearch's three applicability conditions). The bound is a property of the claim, stated once; it is not a defense spread through the text.

7. **Filter honestly.** Cutting *scope* is healthy (TensorFlow dropping TensorBoard; Spanner: "we will not go into detail about the query language"). Cutting a *qualification* that makes a claim read stronger is not (AlphaDev's "integrated into LLVM" without the patch's restrictions). Presenting the design in argument order is fine; asserting false causation is not (GFS says its design was "driven by" workload observations; the real driver of the single master was shipping fast, and that the team controlled every client was a load-bearing condition the paper never stated).

8. **Defend in the work, not in the prose.** The checks in this skill (straw-believer and attack-surface checks, the null-idea test, reviewer simulation, the ledgers) exist to change the work: a smaller claim, a new experiment, a stronger baseline, a stated scope. Their output is a right-sized claim stated plainly, not text arguing with an imagined reviewer. A paper that qualifies, disclaims, re-defines and re-justifies its claims, or keeps saying what it is not, spends its attention defending a framework instead of developing the research. State the decisive limitation once, where it changes belief. State scope once, as the conditions under which the idea works. Credit predecessors by what you build on, not by disclaiming novelty.

## The work

The stages below are the decisions this work has to make, roughly in the order each constrains the next. They are not a template; expect to loop, especially among 2–4 and 7. Frames in the classic cases were usually settled late and more than once. Spend most of your attention on stages 1–4, where the paper is decided. The checks, ledgers, and diagnostic catalogs in later stages and in the references are aids for finding defects; running them exhaustively is no substitute for holding the whole system, the field, and the whole argument in view.

### 1. Read the system for decisions, not components

Read everything first: the design materials, earlier drafts if any, and the relevant literature. The reading produces material for the paper (what this system can contribute and with what evidence), not the paper's problem; that comes from the field (stage 2). Docs and repos are organized by component and chronology. Paper-worthy ideas live in *decisions*. Look for:

- **Fan-out decisions**: one choice that many capabilities depend on. Count what would disappear if it were reversed. MapReduce: determinism → safe re-execution → fault tolerance, backup tasks, load balancing. Fan-out 1 is a feature; fan-out ≥3 is a thesis candidate.
- **Departures from the obvious design**, where a reader expects an alternative (GFS: no client cache, 64 MB chunks, single master).
- **Things removed** (Ceph sheds allocation tables): crisp, locatable claims. Check it is a real removal, not a renaming.
- **Stated invariants and contracts**, and the conditions under which they weaken.
- **Interfaces that stayed stable while implementations churned** (visible in git history: low-churn API files over rewritten internals).
- **Surprising measurements**, especially one where a believed proxy and the real outcome come apart (FlashAttention: more FLOPs, less memory traffic, faster).
- **A change of unit**: per iteration, per block, per memory access.
- **The price the system charges its users** (pure functions, static shapes, tolerating duplicate records). Often the honest form of the idea.
- **Dead ends that produced a rule** (Raft's abandoned ranking-based elections).

Signals of necessary-but-not-argument engineering: breadth (model zoos, frontends, backends), operability without a number, ecosystem tooling, parameter defaults, what the environment provides. This material often drove adoption; route it to a companion (docs, blog, later paper) rather than treating it as unimportant.

Guard against two kinds of capture. **Structural capture**: build the claim map before any outline; if renaming the repo's top-level directories or the design package's chapters would change your outline, the outline was captured. **Conceptual capture**, the stronger kind because it looks like an idea: systems often come with their own conceptual scaffolding (named layers, a mnemonic set, an analogy to cognition or biology, a coined unit of work), and requesters often supply favored concepts in the brief. Treat both as hypotheses to investigate, not as the paper's frame. Test: would the problem statement or the section structure change if that vocabulary were removed? If so, it is driving the narrative. Keep a term only where it names a construction the reader needs, and introduce it there.

Docs answer "what is it" and "how to extend it", rarely "why this and not that"; the why has to be rebuilt from design reviews, reverted commits, old versions, and the people. A reading order that puts discovery before verification, after first pinning the version the paper will describe: external claims (READMEs, launch posts) → guarantees and non-goals → alternatives and dead ends (design reviews, RFCs, reverts) → surprising measurements (dashboards, postmortems) → code, to confirm chosen mechanisms → the people, for the history no document records.

→ `references/reading-a-system.md` for the full method, including what to mine from git history and what to ask the authors.

### 2. Formulate the research problem from the field

The system tells you what was built. It does not tell you what problem the paper addresses. Formulate the problem from the development of the field, read independently of the system's account of itself.

- **Read the field's progress, with normal literature.** For the two or three lines of work the problem sits in, trace what each major approach achieved, which concrete problem it solved, and what its success made possible or exposed. Derive the opportunity from that progression. The classics open this way: MapReduce from hundreds of special-purpose computations that each re-solved distribution and failure; vLLM from measuring how existing serving systems waste KV-cache memory. An opportunity derived this way is credible because a reader can check it against literature they know. Cite the field's literature, never the internal design documents or earlier drafts.
- **Separate the problem from the means.** The problem is what gets improved, and for whom. Search, learning, recursion, a decision procedure, an IR, a generative model are means. Test: would the problem statement still hold if a different technique solved it? If it names your technique, it describes your solution. A technique that occurs at every scale of computing defines no problem by itself: a problem framed as "making better decisions" also covers countless phenomena plainly outside the work.
- **Separate the motivating domain from the object of study.** The domain explains why the problem matters (genomics pipelines, a trading platform, large-model serving). The subject is the computational object the system improves: a class of computations, a resource, a workload shape. Scope the subject so the result transfers to other domains that share the object, and keep the domain visible as motivation rather than dropping it.
- **Organize prior work by precise, constructive categories**: what object each line of work improves, what information it uses, which problem it solves. A good taxonomy places concrete, named systems, explains which problem each solves, and exposes the coupling or opening the new work uses; it can then organize the system sections too. Avoid trend sentences ("increasingly", "a growing body of"), "X is doing Y" summaries, categories defined by novelty or provenance, and claims that all prior systems lack one property. None of these gives the reader anything to check.
- **Keep the system's internal vocabulary out of the problem** (stage 1, conceptual capture).

Write the problem in two or three sentences without the system's name, its internal terms, or your technique's name. If you cannot, keep reading the field before choosing a thesis.

### 3. Find what the work changes, and the candidate theses

Write, in one sentence each: what the work changes against the field's current state (a belief shown false or incomplete, a tradeoff dissolved, a requirement met, a quantity recast), with a citation for that state; the **stated gap**; and the **real gap**. In nearly every classic the stated gap sits one abstraction level above the real one. That elevation is how an engineering fact becomes a claim others can use, and it is legitimate only if the evidence reaches the higher level. Check the state you are changing: can you cite someone who holds it, would they recognize your statement of it, and did the field hold this view or did your design need it? (RDD's "DSM" category was defined broadly enough to leave the practical alternative, MapReduce plus caching, out of the comparison.) Beware the conjunction gap ("no existing system has A, B, and C"): it is the weakest frame and the commonest manufactured gap, because it silently claims that composing existing systems fails; the experiment it owes is that failing composition (Ray asserted it and never ran it).

Generate thesis candidates by several independent routes and look for agreement: trace the headline capability back to the decision it depends on; find the decision an outsider would call a mistake that the team defends; find the distinction engineers use to explain behavior (narrow vs wide dependencies; consistent vs defined); look at what got promoted or demoted across versions; check what the evidence can isolate; explain it aloud to someone with no context and see what they repeat back.

Test each candidate:

| Test | Question |
|---|---|
| Name removal | Can it be stated without the system's name or its internal vocabulary? |
| Means removal | Can the problem be stated without naming the technique that solves it? |
| Object | Is the subject a computational object, with the application domain as motivation? |
| Reimplementation | Would someone rebuilding from the paper get the benefit? |
| Refutability | Is there a number or condition that could prove it wrong? |
| Ancestry | Is it new against *named* ancestors, with what it builds on credited? |
| Explanatory reach | Does it explain several mechanisms, and generate the section structure? |
| Isolation | Can an experiment separate its effect from incidental engineering? |
| Layer | Is it stated at the WHAT (interface/specification) or HOW (implementation) layer? |

Passing the tests is necessary, not sufficient: a thesis that passes name removal but comes with no literature and no construction is an essay, not a systems paper.

Elevated concepts come in recognizable kinds: a restriction that buys properties (MapReduce, RDD), an interface exposing something hidden (TrueTime), an imported analogy (PagedAttention as virtual memory), a swapped metric (IO-awareness), a problem formulation (TensorGame), a priced non-performance property (Raft's understandability, PyTorch's "10% slower is acceptable, 100% is not"), a premise register (GFS), a synthesis with a requirements vocabulary (Dynamo), organization by reader concern with a unifying metric (Borg), a taxonomy of a gap (Paxos Made Live). Two forms cut across these kinds: subsumption ("X expresses Y"), only as strong as its definition of "express", and a delta against a predecessor the authors own (TensorFlow OSDI against DistBelief's parameter server). Strong papers often combine two kinds: typically one earns attention and the other earns belief (TrueTime's exposed interface plus a four-line proof). The kinds are lenses for asking what a claim needs and how it fails. They are not forms for the prose, and none requires coining a term or building a conceptual system. A new system may fit none; then derive its needs directly (what must the reader believe, what could refute it, what does it cost) rather than forcing a kind.

The tests judge candidates; they do not produce them. When no candidate survives, produce more rather than lowering the bar. Generative moves that work on raw material: write the one-paragraph explanation you would give a strong engineer from an adjacent field, then look at which sentence they would find surprising; take the system's most surprising measurement and ask what belief it contradicts; take the price the system charges its users and ask what that restriction buys; take the decision the team defends hardest and state the world in which it would be wrong; describe the system in a neighboring field's vocabulary (operating systems, databases, compilers, numerical methods) and see whether the mapping is near-isomorphic. Write several candidate thesis sentences quickly and badly before testing any; one codebase can support several honest theses, so the first one written is rarely the best.

### 4. Choose the frame, the genre, and the venue fit

Not every system has a single idea, and forcing one produces a slogan. Decide honestly which genre the system supports: an **idea paper** (one concept, mechanism, isolating evidence); a **synthesis** (known techniques combined, with the kind of contribution stated plainly, once, as in Dynamo); a **concern-organized** paper about a mature revealed system (Borg, held together by a cross-cutting metric and paired benefit/cost sections); an **experience** paper (organized by gap category, failures quantified, abstractions extracted, as in Paxos Made Live); a **system-as-instrument** paper where the headline is a discovery and the system is demoted to Methods (AlphaTensor, FunSearch); a **measured-system** paper whose contribution is operational data and where inherited assumptions broke (the thesis HDFS's paper had in its own data but never claimed); or **several papers**, when candidate ideas need different evidence environments (Ceph split placement, replication, and the file system). The list is not exhaustive. The diagnostic is not "is there one idea?" but "is there any organizing principle other than the code's module boundaries?" `references/reading-a-system.md` §9 maps what the reading found to the genre it supports.

Venues reward different currencies: a transferable abstraction with broad evaluation (SOSP/OSDI), deployment lessons without novelty (operational tracks), principle plus theorem plus quality result (NeurIPS), scientific significance verifiable without the system (Nature). Read a few recently accepted papers at the target venue and note what counts as a contribution, where related work sits, which evidence type carries the claim, and how much explicit scoping its papers carry.

Generate two or three competing frames. For each, write at least the problem statement, what the work changes, and the decisive experiment, plus the venue and layer where frames differ on them. Then do a **trial by abstract**: draft an abstract for each using only claims that are measured or argued. A frame whose key sentences need asserted claims is out, or needs a new experiment. Freeze a frame once it passes and its decisive experiment is in hand or scheduled; reopen only for a named reason: new evidence, venue change, reviewer pressure, the system changing under the paper, or the authors rejecting the conception itself. A rejected conception calls for re-conception from stage 2, not patching.

→ `references/finding-the-contribution.md` (competing frames, trial by abstract, reframing triggers, splitting into several papers) and `references/evidence-and-venue.md` (venue currencies, contribution types, maturity, openness).

### 5. Build the argument and control altitude

Every successful paper places each paragraph on a path the reader can follow: **problem** → a small **middle layer** of reasons → **ideas and the mechanisms that realize them** → **capabilities** → **evidence**. Section order, figures, examples, and tables are devices for making that path visible and short. The system enters as the construction that develops the ideas, after the problem and ideas are in place; papers that read as documentation are the ones with no such path. State research ideas in the field's vocabulary and introduce each mechanism together with the idea it serves; a contribution list pairing each component with a capability is the design catalog in bullet form.

- **The middle layer** connects the problem to the mechanisms: two to five observed difficulties of the problem, premises about workload or environment stated with numbers, priced principles, named invariants, or one metric. It says why the construction has the shape it has; it is not an axiom system. Draw each item from something observed in the field or the system (a measurement, a premise, a difficulty practitioners hit), never from a definition you introduced. An item earns its place only if it excludes something a reasonable designer would do *and* a later paragraph uses it as the reason for a choice. Do not build a closed chain of defined concepts → derived requirements → a design shown to satisfy each requirement; that reads as an exercise in deduction, makes the paper defend its definitions, and hides the problem. The middle layer may stay open: difficulties the construction addresses only partly are part of a research account. Generic principles ("simple, scalable, flexible") give no leverage.
- **Describe each layer only through its neighbors**: the layer above says why it exists, the layer below says what it is made of. Lower layers (the file system you build on, the closed RPC substrate) appear by the contract you use, with the one dependency that can take you down quantified (Bigtable on Chubby: 0.0047% unavailability).
- **A mechanism earns full treatment** if it is a link in the chain to a claimed capability, has an alternative the reader expects, has a number attached, or separates you from a named rival. Put the *why* in the sentence next to the *what*, not in a separate rationale section. Where a reader genuinely expects a different design, useful patterns are "choice → benefits → cost → why the cost hasn't bitten" (GFS) and "textbook version → what breaks here → our variant" (Dynamo). A mechanism the reader has no reason to doubt needs its role in the construction explained, not a defense.
- **Demote, don't silently drop.** A named "Refinements" bucket keeps the core readable. An Implementation section's role depends on the claim type: when the claim is "this abstraction enables these capabilities" it holds necessary facts (size, language, substrate, lineage) and stays short; when the claim is "this can be implemented at acceptable cost" (PyTorch) or the runtime is where the chain is shown (MapReduce), it *is* the argument and needs matching experiments. Either way it must not introduce a new organizing idea. A one-sentence dead end shows design maturity.
- **Ordering**: concept first when the reader can see what the idea removes without knowing internal structures (Bigtable's data model, vLLM's paging analogy); substrate first, trimmed to exactly the objects the idea acts on, when the idea's value is simplifying structures the reader hasn't met (Spanner). Outside-in (user view before internals) fixes the capability vocabulary early.
- **Concreteness, without an artificial example.** Use an example to make mechanisms concrete *after* the problem has been stated in its own terms, never as the paper's motivation or a substitute for the research problem. Take it from a real workload, a canonical instance from the field, or a real configuration, so it has significance of its own; introduce it where the first mechanism needs it. A good one forces each central mechanism to fire and exposes at least one cost (vLLM's prompt tracked block by block through allocation, fork, and copy-on-write; Dynamo's shopping cart, Amazon's own service, where deleted items resurface). Test: delete the example; if the problem is no longer stated anywhere, the example had replaced it. Invariants with a short proof, a canonical small instance (Strassen's 2×2 for AlphaTensor), or distributions over many real instances can carry concreteness instead. Removing a toy example does not excuse vague mechanisms: the real construction still has to be concrete enough to re-implement its key idea.
- **Figures argue**: write the one sentence each figure argues before drawing it. Figures do six jobs: roadmap (boxes labeled with section numbers), separation (control vs data flow), thesis in one picture, a bridge where proxy and outcome come apart, the user-facing contract, and robustness built into the plot type (CDFs over many clusters). More than one "here are the components" figure is inventory.
- **Indexes must agree**: abstract, contributions, overview figure, and evaluation questions are different cuts of the same argument for different readers. Check privately with a contributions × sections × experiments matrix: empty cells are defects, and a contribution that points to every section is the system, not an idea. The matrix never appears in the paper, and coherence is the goal, not closure: open questions stated as such are part of a research account.

### 6. Filter and tier the material

For every true, substantial thing about the system, decide where it goes: on the causal chain (full treatment) → refinements bucket → implementation section → one-sentence demotion placed where a reader would actually wonder → appendix/Methods/SI → companion paper → docs, blog, or README → nowhere. Rules the classics actually used: keep links on the causal chain to the thesis; drop what would need a second organizing idea; keep what differs from a named alternative or has a number; hold lower layers fixed as environment, unless a lower-layer property is load-bearing for a claim, in which case it is a premise and must be stated (GFS's relaxed consistency worked because the team controlled every client); keep what a reader can verify without the system; keep a few small failures that motivated choices. Papers tend to drop the failures that *challenge* a premise; those belong next to the premise they break.

Two checks that matter most:

- **Does the cut change the truth of any claim?** If so, it is a cut of qualification. Put it back, inside the sentence that makes the claim, once.
- **What would a skeptical practitioner use instead?** List three. Any that the work has not engaged (measured, or understood well enough to say precisely which different problem it solves) is a future attack surface (MapReduce never discussed relational databases; the critique came three years later). In the paper, the alternative appears where it illuminates the problem or a design choice, described by what it does well, not as a paragraph pre-empting an objection.

Fidelity to the design documents is not the paper's job. Honesty constrains what the paper says about the system (reality status, causation, attribution, release contents); it does not require the paper to mirror the design package, adopt its terms, cover its components, or show correspondence to it. Check privately that nothing in the paper describes something that does not exist.

Decide where demoted material lives *before* cutting. What a flagship paper omits becomes the system's public identity until another document corrects it (Spanner needed a 2017 paper to say "today's Spanner is very different from what was described there").

→ `references/filtering-and-honesty.md` for the rules, destinations, honest vs revisionist narrative, and disclosure of what was withheld.

### 7. Design the evidence as claim tests

The paper's real claim is whatever its evidence tests. Classic papers repeatedly measured the whole system and attributed the result to the idea. The gaps took three shapes: *attribution* (the number is real, the cause is other engineering: Spark's 20× came from avoiding HDFS reads, text parsing, and deserialization, not from lineage), *scope* (the claim covers a regime the evaluation doesn't reach: Spanner's "global" microbenchmarks ran with zones <1 ms apart), and *property* (the claimed adjective has no instrument: TensorFlow's "flexibility"). Type each claim (existence, mechanism attribution, magnitude in a regime, parity, generality, non-performance property, guarantee, use), because the type decides what can test it. Map every claim to the experiment that tests it, and every figure back to a claim. Then:

- **Apply the null-idea test** to each experiment, especially the ones behind abstract numbers: would the result change if the central idea were false but all other engineering stayed the same? If not, the experiment tests the system, not the idea. Decompose it, move the number off the idea, or run the experiment that does change. Production material makes this the default case: internal docs report results per rollout ("p99 dropped 3× after the launch"), and a rollout usually bundles several changes.
- **Make the central idea, not incidental engineering, what the evidence isolates.** In rough order of directness: toggle the mechanism inside the same system (MapReduce: backup tasks off → 44% slower); decompose your own headline before a reviewer does (RDD's breakdown of its speedup); show proxy and outcome coming apart (FlashAttention: more FLOPs, less memory traffic, faster); measure the mechanism alone, then the whole (PyTorch's per-mechanism traces); compare against the design a competent engineer would build in a week *without* the idea, the comparison the classics most often skipped and where critics struck.
- **When the work dissolves a tradeoff, measure the side that was supposed to lose** (PyTorch: within 17% of the fastest framework; Spanner: commit-wait cost; Pathways: single-controller costs nothing at parity).
- **For non-performance claims** (usability, understandability), build a proxy and disclose its bias, narrow the claim to what is measured, and pair it with conventional evidence so the paper stands even if the proxy is discounted (Raft).
- **Choose evidence kinds that fit the claim**: microbenchmarks against hardware limits when no fair baseline exists; end-to-end against the strongest real baseline; production telemetry and fleet distributions for operational claims; trace replay and simulation for policy; proofs and invariants for guarantees, where a claim actually rests on them (formalism added for legitimacy is artificial); user studies (with bias disclosed) for non-performance properties; verifiable artifacts for discoveries.
- **Baselines**: set the baseline bar per claim, not per paper: full rigor for magnitude and attribution claims, less for existence, disclosure, and guarantee claims (GFS and Borg had no competitor baseline and didn't need one; Spark's aging Hadoop baseline and AlphaTensor's missing equal-budget search did cost them). State how any self-built baseline was configured, in the sentence that uses it; report where competitors win.
- **Costs and scope**: pair each benefit with its measured cost. Size each claim so it is true as stated; a right-sized claim needs no trailing caveat. Name, to yourself, the limitation that would most reduce belief in the abstract's first claim: if it holds, shrink the claim, or state the limitation once and plainly where the claim is made. Papers tend to be candid about harmless costs and quiet about this one. State scope once, as the conditions under which the idea works. Do not repeat qualifications, list what the paper is not, or answer objections no reader would raise.
- **Match evidence to maturity and openness.** The younger the system, the more evidence should rest on the WHAT (invariants, mechanism ablations, parity) rather than implementation numbers the next version will change. A closed system makes the paper the artifact: parity on public baselines, published configurations, worst cases, and the contract required of any closed substrate (Pathways). An open system must keep paper and release in agreement. Releasing a checker but not the generator backs claims about *outputs*, not claims about the *method* (efficiency, necessity, which components matter).
- **For each claim without evidence, choose one of three moves**: run the isolating experiment; rescope the claim by writing its condition into the sentence; or move it to the discussion as a stated bet. Rescoping is usually cheaper than an experiment and always better than an assertion. Spend experiments on the decisive uncertainty (attribution, the simplest alternative) before breadth.
- **Unfinished evaluation.** When experiments have not been run, write the evaluation as the questions it will answer and the experiments that will answer them. Do not write numbers, trends, or outcomes that have not been measured, even as placeholders in result-shaped prose, and do not substitute a walkthrough of the design for evidence. The abstract and introduction then claim only what is built or argued.
- **Organize the evaluation by capability or claim, not by dataset or platform**; open with the questions it answers.
- **Reconcile across artifacts**: every public number (blog, README, paper) should state which baseline it answers; every evaluated feature should exist in the cited release.

→ `references/evidence-and-venue.md`.

### 8. Draft

Draft the body before the first page, and write the first page last, against these jobs. Motivate the problem and say why it matters now. Place it in the field's progress: what existing work achieved and the opportunity that leaves, with real citations and precise categories. State the problem and the thesis early (GFS by sentences 3–4; MapReduce within about 15 lines; Ray's paragraph 7 is the slow end). Disclose reality status (implemented? deployed? at what scale?) in one plain sentence. Give a map only when the order is unusual. Do not open with an invented scenario, a list of what the paper is not, or an announcement of which systems serve as examples; develop the account directly.

The abstract's grammar works best when the **idea, as it bears on the problem, is the subject** and the capabilities are its consequences ("TrueTime … is critical to supporting external consistency"), not "we describe the design, implementation, and evaluation of X", and not declarations of what computation or systems "are". Avoid an abstract whose second half walks through components one per sentence.

**Constructive voice.** Develop the ideas through the problem and the construction; do not run a claim → evidence-hunt → qualification → definition cycle. Do not define concepts in order to derive requirements. Do not carry this skill's checks into the prose: no printed ledgers or matrices, no "this section realizes requirement 2", no correspondence to design documents. Give each guarantee once, in the sentence that describes its mechanism, rather than restating it in every section. Avoid protective negations aimed at readings nobody proposed ("X never Y", "this is not A but B", "need not", "only at"). Formalism appears only where a reader uses it (an interface they program against, a guarantee that is proved, a model whose predictions are tested). Coin names sparingly, only for constructions or quantities the reader will refer to repeatedly; slogans, triads, and brand names for the paper's stance are packaging. When helpful, use one name for the idea (IO-aware) and one for the artifact (FlashAttention), with the idea's name on the component that produces the benefit.

**Titles** state the object and the direction of improvement ("Fast and Memory-Efficient Exact Attention with IO-Awareness"; "In Search of an Understandable Consensus Algorithm"; "An Automated End-to-End Optimizing Compiler for Deep Learning"). Avoid slogans, internal terms, a means in the title's main slot, and neutral process words ("evolving", "rethinking", "towards") that name no direction.

**Related work.** Place it early when the reader's existing systems define the problem, late when the difference lies in premises the reader must learn first. Describe each line of prior work by the problem it solved and what its success made possible, grouped by the same precise categories as stage 2. Credit the nearest respected predecessor in the form of what this work builds on and adds (TVM: Halide's compute/schedule separation, "also separating target hardware intrinsics"). Do not end every paragraph by attaching the prior work to your system, or dismiss each system by one deficiency. Lessons sections earn their place only when each lesson is tied to a reversed decision or a measurement; write each as claim → evidence → consequence (Borg puts the bad lessons first, which reads as candor).

→ `references/constructive-narrative.md` §5.2 (defensive patterns and their constructive rewrites), §5.3 (what stays mandatory and how to say it once), §7 (concreteness), §8 (names and titles).

### 9. Diagnose and revise

Revision in the classics meant section-level chops, not sentence edits; when a conception fails, write anew from the problem rather than surface-revising. Diagnose by symptom, but trace each symptom upstream before fixing it locally; patching a symptom of a wrong frame is filling a wrong form well. Fix top-down: frame, then structure, then evidence, then voice, then wording (voice defects that come from the conception, such as design-led order, deduction-driven structure, internal vocabulary as frame, or an artificial example, rank with the frame); rank defects by how much they threaten belief in the abstract's first claim. When revising to feedback, keep earlier directions that are still valid: point-by-point compliance with the latest round tends to drop what that round did not mention. When reviewing someone else's draft, report what only the authors can answer (the real decision history, whether an unstated reason was load-bearing) as questions, not as defects. Key probes:

- The sections follow the design documents' components or chronology, even under concept titles → design-led; re-derive the order from problem → ideas → construction.
- The problem statement names a technique, or the scope is an application domain while the mechanism acts on a general computation → means or domain taken for the problem.
- Concepts are defined, requirements derived, and the design shown to satisfy each → deduction-driven; replace with observed difficulties.
- Disclaimers, the same caveat in several places, sentences announcing how to read the paper → defense; delete each and check whether any claim became false. If none did, it was defense; if one did, resize that claim.
- Prior work is said to lack one property, or is described by trend labels → manufactured gap.
- An invented scenario in the introduction is carried through the paper → artificial example standing in for the problem.
- A mechanism a reader would expect to be done differently appears with no alternative and no cost → description, not argument. (A mechanism with no expected alternative needs only its role.)
- A figure maps to no claim, or a claim has no figure; numbers appear that were never measured.
- The thesis can't be stated without the system's name or its internal vocabulary.
- No mechanism is concrete enough to re-implement the core idea.
- The named concept is a renaming, a slogan, or names a component that isn't where the benefit comes from.
- The paper's best data or candor sits in its last two sections.

Two private meta-tests: the **three-lists test** (contributions, sections, figures side by side; orphans in any direction are defects) and the **rewrite test** (which sentences would survive the system's next major rewrite? If the thesis isn't among them, reconsider its layer).

Simulate the pressures that historically reshaped papers before a reviewer applies them: an expert from each adjacent field asking what they'd use instead; a competitor beating your headline record next month with a cheaper method (what remains?); a critic of *significance* as well as of method (AlphaDev answered the method critic with a baseline and was still attacked as "one saved instruction"); a non-specialist reading "algorithm", "first", or "global" at full strength; the system outgrowing the paper. If a simulation produces "we would add X if asked", add X now when X is evidence (an experiment, a baseline, a measured cost). If X is a paragraph rebutting the objection, change the claim instead, or leave it: a rebuttal written in advance reads as defense. Beware defensive accretion across review rounds, where each round adds qualifications and definitions until the research recedes; the fix is to resize claims and delete the defense, not to add a reply to each comment.

→ `references/diagnostics.md` for the full symptom → cause → fix catalog and reviewer-simulation questions. When a draft reads like a catalog and the fix isn't obvious, `references/worked-transformations.md` helps identify which transformation move is missing.

## Working artifacts

All of these are back-of-house. None appears in the paper as such: no ledger, no claim-to-evidence map, no list of requirements with the sections that satisfy them, no account of how the paper corresponds to the design documents. They change what the paper says; they are not what it says. Keep an artifact only while it changes decisions; a perfectly filled ledger for the wrong thesis is still the wrong paper (TensorFlow's OSDI paper was locally excellent and globally mispositioned against what drove adoption).

- **Problem statement**: two or three sentences without the system's name, its internal terms, or the technique's name.
- **Thesis sentences**: two to four candidates early.
- **What the work changes**: the state of the field it advances, with citations. If you cannot cite that state, the gap may be manufactured.
- **Claims ledger**: claim → claim type → mechanism → specific evidence (a figure, table, or proof, not "§6") → status (measured / argued / asserted / future). Mark the one claim the paper cannot lose. Tag generality claims as built-and-measured / built / argued by correspondence / conjecture; only the first two belong in the abstract. Keep it to the paper's 3–6 main claims plus what readers will *infer* from the title and abstract ("global", "first", "unified").
- **Filter ledger**: what's cut, why, where it goes, whether its absence changes any claim's truth, whether it opens an attack surface, and whether it drove adoption (and so needs a home elsewhere). Include alternatives you chose not to engage.
- **Alternatives register**: for each key decision, the alternative a reader would expect, why it was rejected, and whether that reason is *documented history* or *reconstructed analysis*. Do not present reconstructed alternatives as history; "an alternative would be Y; it would cost…" is legitimate analysis in its own voice, used only where a reader would expect it.
- **Premise register**: workload and environment assumptions with numbers. GFS's conclusions aged badly but its method aged well, because readers could see exactly which premise broke.
- **Figure outline**: one claim sentence per planned figure.

**Briefs to other writers or agents** stay principle-level: the problem, the ideas, the constraints of voice, and what is known to have failed. Do not pass this skill's tests, tables, or probes down as acceptance criteria, or prescribe the system's implementation; checklists turn writing into filling in forms.

## What only the authors can decide

Ask early, and bring two or three candidate framings with their evidence needs rather than open questions; people answer "which of these would you defend?" better than "what's your contribution?".

1. The research problem they see the work addressing, in the field's terms; and which of the system's internal concepts (layer names, frameworks, analogies) they consider essential, versus usable only where they explain the construction.
2. Which claim do they want to be held to for years?
3. Target venue, track, and deadline.
4. What evidence exists or can be produced in time (telemetry, traces, ablation-capable builds, failure injection, user studies, access to competitors)? Ask specifically about natural experiments: staged rollouts or canaries where only one change shipped, config history, tickets and migration notes as evidence of use. These are often the cheapest way to close an attribution gap.
5. What is confidential or unnameable (business numbers, internal system names, closed substrates)?
6. Which version the paper describes; what is implemented, deployed, and evaluated; which components are about to be rewritten.
7. The real decision history, and whether any reason (time-to-market, control of all clients, team size) was load-bearing; any known failures that challenge the premises, not just ones that motivated choices.
8. Lineage, credit, prior public claims (blog numbers must agree with the paper or the difference be explained).
9. Which alternative they consider the real competitor, and which omissions their community would punish.
10. Artifact release plans. These decide whether evidence can be "checkable outputs" or must be "behavior we measured", and whether evaluated features must ship.

## Caveats on this skill's own representation

The classics are survivors: influential papers whose success also owed something to authority, scale, access, and timing a new group may not have. Moves that worked for Google, Amazon, or DeepMind (adoption tables in place of baselines, telemetry-only evaluation, unevidenced lessons) are weaker for an unknown group; weight evidence-producing moves (ablations, honest baselines, decomposed speedups, clear scope) above authority-dependent ones. The case study compared papers with what critics later attacked, so its evidence is richest about defense and the checks here lean that way: use them to size claims and design evidence, and do not let them set the paper's voice. Passing every test and probe here is evidence about parts of the draft, not proof the chosen frame is right. Before drafting inside a frame, ask whether it is the right one.
